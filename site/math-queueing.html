Queueing theory: why 80% busy is the cliff

A server that is 80% busy makes the average request wait four times as long as the work itself takes. Push it to 90% and the wait more than doubles again. Nothing broke. That is what randomness does to a queue, and it is why your connection pool, your thread pool and your autoscaler all contain a number that stops well short of 100.

Plain text is theory. Boxed examples: problem, working, answer.

Where you meet it

Every time a request has to wait for something that is busy, this chapter is running. Four places you set the number yourself:

Every connection pool

A database pool of 10 connections, an HTTP client capped at 100 sockets. Little's Law says how many you need from two numbers you already have. Example 1 sizes one and finds a pool that can never keep up.

Every thread pool and worker count

Web server workers, a consumer group, a pool of CPU cores. Each is a set of servers in front of one queue. Example 5 settles whether one fast worker beats two slow ones, and by how much.

Every autoscaling threshold

Cloud autoscalers add machines when a metric passes a target, and the targets people choose sit near 50 to 70% busy, never 95. Example 3 shows what the last 20% costs and why a target of 85% turns a surge into an outage.

Every p99 that blows up while the mean looks fine

The dashboard says the average is 50 ms. The 99th percentile is 230 ms and climbing. Example 6 shows that ratio is built into the maths, and Example 4 shows what bursts do to it.

The idea in one breath

A queue is anything that takes requests, works on them one or a few at a time, and makes the rest wait. The thing doing the work is the server: a thread, a database connection, a CPU core, a checkout till. Requests arrive at some rate, written λ (lambda, per second), and each takes some service time to finish, written S. The fraction of time the server is busy is its utilisation, ρ (rho): arrivals per second times seconds per arrival, ρ = λ × S.

If arrivals were perfectly regular and every job took exactly the same time, a server at 99% utilisation would never make anyone wait. Real arrivals cluster and real jobs vary, so now and then several requests land while one is being served, and they queue. The idle 20% of an 80%-busy server is not spread evenly through the day. It comes in gaps, and a gap does nothing for the three requests that arrived together just before it.

The result is the one curve in this chapter: the mean wait is S × ρ/(1 − ρ). The denominator 1 − ρ is the headroom, the fraction of time the server is free. Halve the headroom and the wait doubles. Going from 80% to 90% busy cuts the headroom from 0.2 to 0.1, so the wait more than doubles. Approach 100% and the wait heads for infinity. Everything else on this page is that fraction, applied.

The four tools you need

Arithmetic on averages, nothing more.

1. Rates and averages

An arrival rate λ is a count divided by a time. A service time S is an average, in seconds. Its reciprocal μ = 1/S (mu) is the service rate: how many the server could finish per second if it never sat idle. Keep the units honest and the rest follows.

3. Little's Law, L = λW

The average number of things inside a system, L, equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends inside, W. It holds for any system in a steady state, whatever the randomness, and it works for the whole service, for the queue alone, or for the servers alone. Know two of the three and you have the third.

4. Variability as a coefficient of variation

The coefficient of variation is the standard deviation divided by the mean, and the formulas use its square, C². A job that always takes the same time has C² = 0. The exponential distribution, the pattern of gaps between arrivals when each arrival is independent of the last, has a standard deviation equal to its mean, so C² = 1. Anything burstier is above 1.

Sizing a pool with Little's Law

Little's Law is the most useful line in this chapter because it assumes nothing about randomness. It was proved by John Little in 1961, the same year Kingman published the formula in Example 4, so two of the four tools here share a birthday. Point it at a connection pool: the "system" is the set of connections, a request "arrives" when it borrows one and "leaves" when it hands it back.

One server, random arrivals

Queueing theorists name a queue by its arrivals, its service times and its server count, a shorthand David Kendall introduced in 1953. M/M/1 means arrivals are memoryless (M, for Markov: each arrival is independent of the last, so the gaps between them are exponential), service times are memoryless too, and there is one server. It is the simplest queue that has any randomness in it, and it has an exact answer. The mean time spent waiting in the queue, before service starts, is

Wq = S × ρ / (1 − ρ), and the mean response time is T = Wq + S = S / (1 − ρ).

We will not derive it. We will use it, and then measure it in "Run it" to see that it is true.

At 50% each request waits one service time. At 80% it waits four, at 90% nine, at 99% ninety-nine. Response time is S/(1 − ρ): 2, 5, 10, 20 and 100 times the work itself. The server never did anything more slowly. It was only busy more often.

The cliff

The curve's slope is 1/(1 − ρ)²: the wait grows with the square of the shrinking headroom. That is why the same ten points of load cost almost nothing at the left of the chart and everything at the right, and it is the whole argument for autoscaling before a machine is busy.

Bursts and slow requests

M/M/1 assumes both arrivals and service have C² = 1. Real traffic is usually burstier than that, and real service times often have a slow minority. John Kingman's 1961 approximation puts variability into the formula as a separate factor:

Wq ≈ ρ/(1 − ρ) × (Ca² + Cs²)/2 × S

Three factors: the load, the variability (arrivals Ca² and service Cs², averaged), and the service time. With Ca² = Cs² = 1 the middle factor is 1 and you get M/M/1 back. It is exact whenever arrivals are memoryless, whatever the service times look like, and a close approximation otherwise, closest at high load, which is where you care.

One fast server or two slow ones

Two servers can share one queue, written M/M/2: whoever is free next takes the next request. Its mean response time has a closed form too, T = S / (1 − ρ²), where S is one server's service time and ρ = λS/2 is each server's utilisation. Compare it with a single server twice as fast, and with the same two servers behind a load balancer that splits traffic at random so each keeps its own queue.

Two separate queues are exactly twice as slow as one fast server at every load, and worse than the shared queue at every load: if you have two servers, share the queue. What the formula does not count is that when one of the two slow servers dies you still have half a service, and when the fast one dies you have none. Harchol-Balter's textbook opens with this exact question and works the three layouts through in its chapter on server farms.

Why p99 explodes while the mean looks fine

In an M/M/1 queue the response time is not just on average S/(1 − ρ). Its whole distribution is exponential with that mean, and the exponential has a long tail. The fraction of requests slower than t is e−t/mean, so the pth percentile sits at mean × ln(1/(1 − p)). Those logarithms are fixed numbers: the median is 0.69 of the mean, the 99th percentile is 4.6 times the mean, at any load. What the load does is move the mean, and the mean is on the cliff.

Run it

Build it: one server, half a million requests

Standard library only. Draw a random gap, draw a random service time, and a request waits whenever the server is still busy from the last one. Then compare the measured wait with Example 2 and Example 4.

# One server, random arrivals, random service: measure the wait, then compare
# with the formula. Companion to math-queueing.html. Python 3 stdlib only.
import random, statistics

S = 10.0          # mean service time, ms
N = 500_000       # arrivals per run

def simulate(rho, bursty=False, seed=1):
    """Return mean wait, median and 99th percentile of response time, in ms."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    lam = rho / S                                   # arrivals per ms
    def gap():
        if not bursty:
            return rng.expovariate(lam)             # Poisson arrivals: Ca^2 = 1
        # Bursty: 89% of gaps are short, 11% are long. Same mean, Ca^2 = 4.
        if rng.random() < 0.8873:
            return rng.expovariate(1.7746 * lam)
        return rng.expovariate(0.2254 * lam)
    now = free_at = 0.0
    waits, resp = [], []
    for _ in range(N):
        now += gap()                                # next request arrives
        start = max(now, free_at)                   # wait if the server is busy
        free_at = start + rng.expovariate(1 / S)    # exponential service, mean S
        waits.append(start - now)
        resp.append(free_at - now)
    resp.sort()
    return statistics.fmean(waits), resp[N // 2], resp[int(N * 0.99)]

print(f"S = {S:.0f} ms, {N:,} requests per run, one server")
print("rho   formula  measured   p50    p99   (ms)   Poisson arrivals")
for rho in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95):
    w, p50, p99 = simulate(rho)
    print(f"{rho:.2f}  {S * rho / (1 - rho):7.1f}  {w:8.1f}  {p50:5.1f}  {p99:6.1f}")

print("rho   kingman  measured   p50    p99   (ms)   bursty arrivals, Ca^2 = 4")
for rho in (0.5, 0.8, 0.9):
    w, p50, p99 = simulate(rho, bursty=True)
    print(f"{rho:.2f}  {S * rho / (1 - rho) * (4 + 1) / 2:7.1f}  {w:8.1f}  {p50:5.1f}  {p99:6.1f}")

Multiply each throughput by its time per request: 2,379 × 0.000420 = 1.0, then 2,275 × 0.004396 = 10.0, then 2,289 × 0.021843 = 50.0. That is Little's Law, L = λW, read straight off a load test: the tool holds exactly c requests in flight, so c equals throughput times latency, every time. The server's throughput did not move, because one Python process can only do so much, so the extra 49 clients bought nothing but a queue: 52 times the latency and a p99 of 96 ms against 1. The last two lines are the queue you never configured. When a connection has finished its TCP handshake but the process has not yet called accept(), the operating system holds it in the listen backlog, which on this machine is 128 deep. Beyond that, connections are refused. It is a bounded queue with load shedding built in, and every server you have ever run sat behind one.

Toy vs production

The formulas are exact for the model. Production differs from the model in specific, nameable ways, and each one has a standard response.

This pageReal systems
arrivalsmemoryless, steady rateA daily cycle, so capacity is planned for the peak hour, not the mean. Bursts, so Kingman's Ca² is above 1 and the cliff arrives early (Example 4). Retries, which are arrivals that grow as you slow down: the feedback loop behind most cascading failures, and why retry budgets and backoff exist.
service timememoryless, one meanMixtures: cache hits at 1 ms and misses at 50 ms, a garbage-collection pause every few seconds. Cs² above 1 makes the fast requests wait behind the slow ones, so services split big jobs, cap request sizes and move slow work to a separate queue.

the queueno limitBounded everywhere: the 128-deep listen backlog, a pool's checkout timeout, a thread pool's queue length. When full, fail fast rather than wait: a request that waits 5 s and then times out cost the server 5 s of a slot for nothing. Load shedding is the deliberate version.
utilisation targeta number you pickAutoscaling targets of 40 to 70% on CPU or requests per target; AWS's worked example is 50%. New capacity takes minutes to launch and warm up, so the target is the load you can survive for those minutes, not the load you can serve today.
the tailp99 = 4.6 × mean, one serverFan-out multiplies the tail (Example 6). In Google's measurement a single random backend answered within 10 ms at its p99, but waiting for all of them made the p99 140 ms. Fixes: hedged requests to a second replica after the p95 time, per-request deadlines that are passed downstream, and treating the slowest 1% of a fleet as a signal rather than noise.
